# Product Case Study: TenderFlow to Open Tenders

## Problem

Bid teams coordinate complex submissions across spreadsheets, inboxes, calendars and individual memory. This makes qualification, ownership, deadline risk and learning difficult to see in one place.

## Users

The product was designed for small UK bid teams, operational contributors and leaders who need pipeline visibility without administering a heavyweight enterprise proposal platform.

## MVP decision

The MVP focused on shared tender records, ownership, deadlines and stage progression. Kanban, calendar and dashboard views sit over the same records because they support different decisions without creating separate sources of truth.

Multi-user organisations and roles were included early: a bid tracker does not become a team product until ownership and permissions are credible.

## Delivered capabilities

- Tender records, qualification and workflow stages
- Ownership, deadlines and risk flags
- Dashboard, Kanban and calendar views
- Multi-user organisations and permissions
- Pipeline and win/loss insights
- Sites and staffing map
- Optional reminder integration

## Commercial pivot

The original product, TenderFlow, was intended for commercialisation. Market review showed a crowded tender- and proposal-management category without enough evidence that the proposition was differentiated.

Rather than treating completed code as a reason to continue, the commercial effort stopped and the product became Open Tenders, an open-source edition.

## What remains unproven

The public project demonstrates a working product and informed workflow design. It does not yet demonstrate repeat adoption, willingness to pay or a defensible route to market.

## Next experiment

Observe five bid professionals completing the same scenario: add an opportunity, qualify it, assign an owner, identify the next deadline and explain the largest risk. Measure task completion, time, terminology problems and information that remains outside the product.

## Lesson

Domain experience can reveal a real problem, but commercial product decisions still require evidence about differentiation, behaviour change and willingness to pay.
